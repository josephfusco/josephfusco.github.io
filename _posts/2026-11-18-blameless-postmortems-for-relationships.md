When software breaks, good teams run a blameless postmortem to understand what happened so it does not happen again. John Allspaw [wrote about this at Etsy in 2012](https://www.etsy.com/codeascraft/blameless-postmortems) ([archived](https://web.archive.org/web/20261025192059/https://www.etsy.com/codeascraft/blameless-postmortems)): when people fear blame, they hide details, and everyone learns less.

I am learning to use the same format in relationships.

It starts with waiting. Teams do not run a postmortem during an outage. In [*The Seven Principles for Making Marriage Work*](https://www.gottman.com/product/the-seven-principles-for-making-marriage-work/) ([archived](https://web.archive.org/web/20261022064518/https://www.gottman.com/product/the-seven-principles-for-making-marriage-work/)), John Gottman and Nan Silver write that when we are flooded, heart racing, we cannot take in what the other person is saying. They suggest a break of at least 20 minutes. This part is hard, and it takes a commitment from both people.

The postmortem itself is five questions.

1. What happened?
2. What was each person seeing?
3. Where did the signals cross?
4. What made it worse?
5. What will I do differently?

Answer the first with facts only, dates and numbers: "Tuesday at 6 and Thursday at 9" beats "a couple times." The second is there because two people can see the same moment differently. Signals cross in words, in timing and in silence, and stress, old hurts and missing context make it worse. The last answer is one or two changes I own.

Here is an example. I said I would be home by 6. I got home at 7:30 and did not text. I was stuck at work, thinking "I'll explain when I get there." A partner saw 90 minutes of silence and worried. To me the silence meant "busy." To a partner it meant "something's wrong." Stress made me lose track of time, and my phone was in my bag. Next time I text as soon as plans change: "Running late, home by 7:30. Sorry."

Gottman calls criticism one of the Four Horsemen, the patterns that predict breakups. Criticism attacks the person: "You never listen." A complaint names a behavior: "I felt unheard when you checked your phone while I was talking." A postmortem stays on behavior and the conditions around it. You still own your part, without shame, so you can change.

You can run one on your own. Write down what you learned. So far mine come down to three things: telling a partner before they have to ask, giving the real picture ("home by 7:30" beats "a little late"), and sending a text so worry does not fill the gap.
